[[fmt:yellow]]Brummie[[/fmt]] accents 'worse than staying silent', study shows

[[fmt:yellow]]Vocabulary[[/fmt]]		[[fmt:underline]]Expre[[/fmt]][[fmt:underline]]ssions[[/fmt]]		[[fmt:pink]]General Knowledge[[/fmt]]

By [[[fmt:underline]]Hannah Furness[[/fmt]]](http://www.telegraph.co.uk/journalists/hannah-furness/), The Telegraph 16th July 2015

§1 - It has long been said that [[fmt:underline]]silence is golden[[/fmt]]. And if you have a Brummie accent, that [[fmt:underline]]old adage[[/fmt]] may prove to be true, research suggests. Speaking in a Birmingham accent is now viewed as being less attractive than staying completely silent, a study has shown.

Academics found people with a Brummie [[fmt:yellow]]twang[[/fmt]] [[fmt:underline]]are better off[[/fmt]] saying nothing at all if they want to succeed in life. The accent, made famous [[fmt:underline]]by the likes[[/fmt]] of [[fmt:pink]]Ozzy[[/fmt]][[fmt:pink]] Osbourne[[/fmt]], [[fmt:pink]]Jasper Carrot[[/fmt]], and [[fmt:pink]]Andrew Chiles[[/fmt]], was received so negatively it was ranked “less intelligent” than silence.

§2 - The University of [[fmt:pink]]South Wales[[/fmt]] conducted the study into dialect and perceived intelligence by getting people to read a passage in three different accents. Lance Workman, professor of psychology, revealed that results showed those who said nothing were considered cleverer than Brummies.

§3 - He added: "We [[fmt:yellow]]paired[[/fmt]] [[fmt:yellow]]random[[/fmt]] accents with faces and asked people [[fmt:yellow]]to rate[[/fmt]] the [[fmt:yellow]]attractiveness[[/fmt]] and intelligence of a young woman when reading a passage in the three accents.  "We also added a silent condition where participants saw the face but did not hear any dialogue.  "We [[fmt:yellow]]randomised[[/fmt]] faces with accents in order [[fmt:yellow]]to control for[[/fmt]] differences between faces.

§4 - "The results suggested that, [[fmt:yellow]]while[[/fmt]] regional accents did not have an effect on the [[fmt:yellow]]perceived[[/fmt]] attractiveness of the speaker, they do have a significant effect on perceived level of intelligence." People [[fmt:yellow]]ranked[[/fmt]] the [[fmt:pink]]Yorkshire[[/fmt]] accent as the most intelligent, followed successively by "[[fmt:pink]]received pronunciation[[/fmt]]" - or [[fmt:pink]]the Queen's English[[/fmt]] - and finally the Birmingham accent.

§5 - The professor said this may be because Britons [[fmt:yellow]]still[[/fmt]] had preconceived stereotypes of people based on their accents and where they are from.  He added: "A number of studies have found negative assumptions with the Brummie accent.  "This may be related to the flat vowels that are associated with it.  "Examples of this include the pronunciation of pie as 'poi' and pint as 'point'.  A well-known Birmingham [[fmt:yellow]]joke[[/fmt]] involves the term 'cup of tea' and [[fmt:yellow]]'kipper[[/fmt]] tie' being mixed up. "[[fmt:yellow]]Flattening[[/fmt]] of the vowels seems to be seen as an indication of low intelligence, despite not being supported by any [[fmt:underline]]empirical evidence[[/fmt]].  "It is merely a negative stereotype.  "In contrast, specific regional accents are often perceived as inferior and belonging to groups [[fmt:yellow]]lacking in[[/fmt]] prestige.  "It is clear that many of those with regional accents are still [[fmt:underline]]hitting a glass ceiling." [[/fmt]]

§6 - Brummie characters portrayed on TV include [[fmt:pink]]Benny from Crossroads[[/fmt]], played by Paul Henry, and [[fmt:pink]]Barry in Auf Wiedersehen Pet[[/fmt]], played by Timothy Spall. [[fmt:pink]]Jasper [[/fmt]][[fmt:pink]]Carrott[[/fmt]], the comedian, has already criticised [[fmt:yellow]]the findings[[/fmt]] of the study, saying he is [[fmt:yellow]]proud[[/fmt]] to be a Brummie. He added: "We have suffered the insults for so long that it’s the proverbial [[fmt:underline]]water off a ducks back[[/fmt]] attitude that gets us through. "The constant [[fmt:yellow]]sneering[[/fmt]] that goes on about this city gives us a stoicism and determination to succeed. "That is often [[fmt:yellow]]overlooked[[/fmt]] by those would [[fmt:underline]]have nothing better to do[[/fmt]] than [[fmt:yellow]]waste[[/fmt]] money on research from which a five-year-old could tell you the outcome. "In five years time, no doubt, some professor [[fmt:yellow]]nerd[[/fmt]] will come up with research that proves [[fmt:underline]]once and for all[[/fmt]] the Birmingham accent is the very reason why the city has been so successful in commerce and innovation."

§7 - The research comes after a report from the Social Mobility and Child Poverty Commission revealed recruiters favour certain accents over others, regardless of academic merit.  Those who spoke the Queen's English were more likely to be hired due to the [[fmt:yellow]]assumption[[/fmt]] of [[fmt:underline]]the [[/fmt]][[fmt:underline,yellow]]posher[[/fmt]][[fmt:underline]] the voice, the higher the IQ[[/fmt]].  Although only spoken by three per cent of the population, received pronunciation is commonly associated with people of a higher social class.

§8 - Birmingham historian Professor Carl Chinn added: "This [[fmt:underline]]so-called[[/fmt]] study continues to reinforce negative stereotypes [[fmt:yellow]]fuelled by[[/fmt]] the [[fmt:yellow]]long-standing[[/fmt]] prejudice against people who speak in a regional accent.  "These things tend to involve very, very low sample numbers and I do not normally respond to them.  "What the Commission needs [[fmt:yellow]]to address[[/fmt]] is the ignorance and prejudice which mistakenly links intellect and attraction with speech pattern.  "Wouldn't it be great if we woke up tomorrow and everyone was judged by what they say, not by the sound of their voice?
